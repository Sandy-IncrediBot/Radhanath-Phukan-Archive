How He Read the Vedas — And How We Should Read AI

Two timelines. One method. 1896 and 2026.

“AI can give you the text. Phukan teaches you how to question it.”

The connection

His scholarship. Our questions.

Radhanath Phukan (1875–1964) brought disciplined inquiry to his study and translation of Indian philosophy. He returned to foundational texts, examined interpretations, placed ideas in conversation with modern physical science, and sought to make difficult concepts accessible in Assamese. His 1960 edition of the Sāṃkhya Kārikā—subtitled “A Treatise on Psycho-physics for Self-realization”—offers a compelling point of departure for thinking about AI today. This page does not claim that Phukan wrote about artificial intelligence. It applies a contemporary method inspired by his documented scholarly practice.

His daughter-in-law Nirupama Phukan proved the method in the next generation — translating Godan into Assamese for a lay reader. Read about her literary legacy on the main archive page.

AI can retrieve, translate, and summarize at extraordinary speed. But fluency is not discernment. We must ask what is sourced, what is assumed, what is analogy, what remains uncertain—and who is responsible for acting.

A modern framework inspired by his practice

The Five-Step Method — From Text to Discernment

A five-step discipline for reading difficult texts—and for evaluating an AI answer. This is a contemporary synthesis, not a protocol Phukan formally named.

01

Source First

Return to the primary text. Separate the original from commentary, paraphrase, and later interpretation.

02

Question the Premise

Reframe the question. What assumptions are hidden inside it? What would the counter-question be?

03

Compare

Place interpretations and traditions side by side. Make disagreement visible rather than smoothing it away.

04

Test

Examine claims against evidence, reason, experience, and the limits of any analogy. Distinguish proof from illustration.

05

Realise

Turn understanding into responsible judgment and action. Ask what follows—and who remains accountable.

Grounded in Phukan’s own preface

Phukan explains that he translated the Sāṃkhya Kārikās and Brahma-Sūtras into Assamese because they had not been available in Assamese for a long time. He also describes using illustrations from modern physical science to explain his points.

The five-step sequence below is our contemporary synthesis of his scholarly practice—not a protocol he formally named.

Reading in practice · Three examples

How He Examined Ideas

These examples show how a question can be opened up, examined, and explained. They are presented as interpretive pathways; disputed conclusions should remain visible rather than being treated as settled consensus.

CASE 01 · CLASSIFICATION

Sāṃkhya: Is it atheistic?

The question

Can a philosophical system be labelled “atheistic” solely because it does not foreground Ishvara?

Phukan’s interpretive move

Examine the roles of Purusha, Prakriti, and liberation within the system instead of relying only on a label inherited from commentators.

What this opens

The system’s structure may complicate a simple classification. Different schools and scholars interpret Sāṃkhya’s relation to theism differently.

AI practice: Ask for the strongest arguments on both sides, the textual basis for each, and what remains disputed. Do not present one interpretation as consensus without evidence.
CASE 02 · CONTINUITY

Rebirth: What does continuity mean?

The question

How does Janmantar Rahasya approach continuity, causation, and subtle impressions (samskara)?

A possible comparison

Memory, habit, unconscious processes, and psychological continuity may offer points of comparison for a modern reader.

The limit

These comparisons do not empirically establish rebirth, nor do they prove that samskara is identical to a psychological mechanism.

AI practice: Ask the model to separate the philosophical account, modern analogy, and empirical evidence. Keep the boundary explicit.
CASE 03 · ACCESS

Gita and Upanishads: Meaning for a lay reader

The question

How can a difficult philosophical text become meaningful beyond a specialist’s Sanskrit vocabulary?

Phukan’s scholarly practice

Translate and explain major texts in Assamese, using accessible language and illustrations to open the ideas to a wider readership.

What must be preserved

Accessibility should not erase context, ambiguity, or differences between the source text and the explanation.

AI practice: Request source text, translation, transliteration where useful, a plain-language explanation, and notes on interpretive choices.
Phukan & AI

Where philosophy meets computation

These connections are interpretive lenses for today—not claims that Phukan anticipated artificial intelligence.

01 · Philosophical lens

Purusha and Prakriti

In classical Sāṃkhya, Purusha is witnessing consciousness; Prakriti encompasses material and mental processes, including Buddhi, Manas, and Ahamkara. AI computation can be explored as a process within the material world.

Whether an AI system has subjective consciousness is a separate, unresolved question. The analogy should not be mistaken for scientific proof.

Question: Does the system possess understanding—or produce outputs that appear understanding-like?
02 · Human responsibility

Viveka in the human loop

Judge AI not only by fluency or apparent helpfulness, but by discernment: source quality, premise, context, uncertainty, and consequences.

Principle: An AI answer is an input to judgment, not a replacement for judgment.
03 · Language and access

Mother-tongue knowledge

Phukan’s Assamese writing made complex philosophical ideas accessible to readers beyond a Sanskrit- or English-reading scholarly audience. Today, language technology can help preserve and access Assamese texts—if it is grounded, reviewed, and community-aware.

Opportunity: Assamese OCR, citation-grounded translation, and linked concepts—with human editorial review.
A philosophical lens · Not a scientific equivalence

Sāṃkhya Concepts Mapped to AI

These comparisons are intended to help visitors ask better questions. They are analogies for discussion—not proof about the architecture or consciousness of AI systems.

Purusha

Witness-consciousness

In the Sāṃkhya framework, Purusha is consciousness as witness. In the AI conversation, it prompts the question: does a system’s apparent cognition entail subjective awareness? This analogy does not settle the question empirically.

Prakriti

Nature and processes

Prakriti includes material and mental processes. Computation, data, model parameters, and generated output can be discussed by analogy as processes in the natural/material domain—not as a literal identification of AI with Prakriti.

Buddhi

Intellect / discernment — Buddhi (বুদ্ধি)

AI may perform tasks that resemble analysis or classification. The comparison should not conflate task performance with the philosophical faculty—or with human judgment.

Manas

Mind / coordination — Manas (মন)

Input processing and coordination can offer a limited analogy. Similar function does not establish that an AI has a mind in the Sāṃkhya sense.

Ahamkara

“I”-making / self-reference — Ahamkara (অহংকাৰ)

A model can produce first-person language or maintain a persona. That behaviour alone is not evidence of a self or subjective experience.

Gunas

Sattva · Rajas · Tamas

As a reflective lens: Is an answer clear and well-grounded? Is it hurried or overactive? Is it obscure or inert? These are questions about output quality—not a claim that models literally possess the Gunas.

Viveka

Discrimination kept in the human — Viveka (বিবেক)

Discernment means distinguishing source from assertion, evidence from analogy, and fluency from reliability. In this page’s central thesis, it remains a human discipline of evaluation and responsibility.

Contemporary conversation: Jonathan Edelmann’s essays explore how Sāṃkhya’s account of mind, body, and consciousness may frame philosophical questions about AI. They are a modern interpretation—not Phukan’s own writing or an empirical verdict on machine consciousness.

An interactive practice

The Phukan Test

Evaluate an AI answer through five disciplines. Your Yes / No / Maybe choices generate targeted follow-up prompts and a practical next-step plan.

Five questions for discernment0 of 5 answered
1. SOURCE — Can I trace the important claims to a source?Check the original or authoritative source and confirm it supports the claim—not just a summary.
2. PREMISE — Is the question framed fairly and correctly?Examine the question’s assumptions and consider a reasonable alternative framing.
3. COMPARE — Have I checked another credible perspective?Compare an independent credible source or interpretation; note important disagreements.
4. TEST — What is evidence, and what is interpretation or analogy?Separate verifiable evidence from inference, analogy, and uncertainty.
5. REALISE — Do I know what follows, and who is accountable?Clarify the conclusion or action, what remains uncertain, and who is responsible.
Rule of use: This is a reflection aid, not a truth certificate. If a material claim cannot be traced to a credible source, do not rely on it. Independently verify consequential decisions.

Your review will guide the next step

Choose an answer for each check. The result will not merely score the response: it will identify the exact follow-up needed, offer a prompt you can use with the AI, and tell you what to verify yourself.

For visitors · Practical pathways

Where This Method Can Be Used

The Phukan Test is not limited to philosophy or translation. Use the same discipline to review AI-generated claims, explanations, recommendations, and decisions in any field.

Student

Review AI explanations, summaries, and study answers. Check cited sources, challenge assumptions, compare interpretations, and make sure you can explain the result in your own understanding.

Educator

Use AI to prepare lesson ideas, examples, or feedback—then verify factual claims, check for bias or omissions, and keep the teacher accountable for what reaches learners.

Researcher

Interrogate AI-assisted literature reviews, summaries, translations, and hypotheses. Trace claims to primary sources, compare competing accounts, and separate evidence from inference.

Professional

Evaluate AI recommendations in your work—whether in engineering, healthcare, law, finance, or another domain. Verify against relevant standards, data, and expert judgment before acting.

Developer

Design AI products with source traceability, uncertainty cues, alternative perspectives, and human review. Test not only whether an answer sounds good, but whether it is grounded and safe for its intended use.

Journalist · Citizen

Check AI-generated news explanations, public claims, and viral content before repeating them. Find original evidence, examine counter-claims, and communicate what is known and what is uncertain.

For high-stakes decisions, this framework is a starting point—not a substitute for qualified review, authoritative evidence, or required professional and regulatory processes.

A grounded thought experiment · Not a historical claim

What Might Phukan Ask ChatGPT Today?

We cannot know what Phukan would have asked. But his documented emphasis on translation, source texts, interpretation, and modern-science illustrations gives us a disciplined way to imagine a useful exchange.

01

Show the original Sanskrit alongside Assamese and English. Identify translation choices.

02

Present the strongest arguments for and against a disputed interpretation—such as whether Sāṃkhya is atheistic.

03

Offer a modern-physics illustration, then explain where the analogy stops.

04

Provide sources and exact passages that can be inspected—not merely a confident summary.

05

Rewrite the explanation in clear Assamese for a lay reader, preserving nuance.

Not “Tell me the truth.”

Comparative inquiry · Critical reading

A Parallel Is a Question—not a Proof

Comparing philosophical ideas with modern science can open valuable questions. But resemblance alone does not establish scientific equivalence, prediction, or proof.

A contemporary Sāṃkhya–AI conversation

Natural and Artificial Intelligence, and Consciousness

In a two-part essay, philosopher of religion Jonathan Edelmann explores how Sāṃkhya’s distinction between materiality, mental processes, and consciousness might frame questions about artificial intelligence. He treats AI as a philosophical problem: what counts as intelligence, what is meant by consciousness, and how might different traditions conceptualize them?

Read it as: a contemporary philosophical interpretation of Sāṃkhya—not as a position authored by Radhanath Phukan, nor as an empirical demonstration that AI is or is not conscious.

Physics and philosophical comparison

Fritjof Capra — The Tao of Physics

Capra’s widely read work places themes from modern physics in conversation with Eastern philosophical and mystical traditions. It can serve as a point of entry into comparative inquiry—and a reminder to ask how a proposed parallel is constructed.

Read it critically: scholarly critiques have questioned whether Capra sufficiently justifies the different interpretations of physics he draws together. A parallel may be suggestive without being a scientific equivalence.
